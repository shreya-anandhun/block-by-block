Field guide · 2026 edition

The web you can own.

Web2 asked you to trust a company's database. Web3 replaces it with a public ledger that thousands of strangers keep a copy of — and nobody can quietly edit. Here is how that actually works, in plain English, including the parts that still don't work well.

01 — The shift

Three webs, one question: who holds the database?

Each era of the web added a verb. Web1 let you read it. Web2 let you write to it. Web3 adds the ability to own something on it — an asset, an identity, a vote — without a company holding the record on your behalf.

Read · Write · Own

The document web. Pages sat on a server, you fetched them, and that was the whole interaction. Publishing meant knowing HTML and renting hosting, so most people were an audience rather than authors.

Shape
Static pages, directories, personal sites
You are
A reader
Who holds it
Whoever runs the server the page lives on
The catch
Almost nobody could publish, and nothing talked to anything else

Read · Write · Own

The platform web, and the one you are almost certainly using right now. Anyone can post, and it is free — because the account, the followers and the archive are entries in a company's private database, and attention is the product.

Shape
Social feeds, marketplaces, SaaS, app stores
You are
A user — and an account that can be suspended
Who holds it
The platform: your posts, your graph, your reach, your reviews
The catch
The rules, the fees and the API can change overnight, and you cannot take the audience with you

Read · Write · Own

The ownership web. The state that matters — who owns what, who voted how, who is owed what — moves out of a private database and into a blockchain that anyone can read and nobody can silently rewrite. Control comes from holding a key, not from holding an account.

Shape
Wallets, smart contracts, tokens, DAOs, on-chain identity
You are
An address — and the only person who can sign for it
Who holds it
Everyone. Thousands of independent nodes keep the same copy
The catch
Slower, costlier, less forgiving. Lose the key and there is no reset link

Reality check. Web3 does not replace Web2 — almost every real product is a hybrid. The interface you are looking at is ordinary HTML served from an ordinary web host. Only the part that has to be ownable and verifiable is worth the cost of putting on a chain.

02 — Hands on

Why a blockchain is hard to lie to

This is not a diagram. Four real blocks are being hashed with SHA-256 in your browser. Mine them, then edit any block's data and watch every block after it break — that is tamper-evidence, and it is the whole trick.

Not mined yet

  1. 01Hash. Every block is fed through SHA-256, producing a 64-character fingerprint. Change one character of the data and the fingerprint changes completely.
  2. 02Link. Each block also stores the previous block's fingerprint. That stored value is what turns a list into a chain.
  3. 03Work. A block only counts as mined when its hash starts with a run of zeros. The only way to find one is to try nonces until you get lucky — that cost is proof of work.
  4. 04Tamper. Edit block #1 and watch block #2 report a broken link. Re-mine block #1 and it is block #3 that breaks next: repairing one block always dislodges the one after it. To rewrite history you have to work forward through the entire chain — faster than the rest of the network adds new blocks. That is the expensive part.

Drag difficulty to 5 and mine again to feel the curve: each extra zero makes the search about 16× harder. Bitcoin currently needs roughly 19–20 leading zeros, which is why it takes the entire network about ten minutes to find one block. Ethereum stopped doing this work in 2022 and now picks proposers by stake instead.

03 — The vocabulary

Six ideas that carry all the weight

Nearly every Web3 headline is one of these six things wearing a costume. Learn them once and the rest of the jargon becomes readable.

01

Decentralisation

No head office. Thousands of independent computers each keep a full copy of the ledger and independently check every rule. To change the record you would have to convince most of them at once, and they have no reason to agree with you.

CostSlow & redundant by design

02

Consensus

How strangers who distrust each other agree on one history. Proof of work makes you spend electricity to propose a block. Proof of stake makes you lock up capital that gets destroyed — "slashed" — if you cheat. Both make lying more expensive than honesty.

EthereumProof of stake since 2022

03

Smart contracts

Programs deployed to an address instead of a server. Every node runs the same code on the same input and gets the same result, so the outcome needs no referee. They can hold and move funds — and once deployed, a bug is usually permanent.

Written inSolidity, Vyper, Rust

04

Tokens & NFTs

A token is just a balance inside a contract's ledger. Fungible ones (ERC-20) are interchangeable, like currency. Non-fungible ones (ERC-721) are numbered and unique — the NFT is the row saying address X owns item #7, not the picture itself.

RememberA token is a record, not a file

05

DAOs

An organisation whose bank account and bylaws are code. Members hold governance tokens, proposals are voted on in the open, and a passing vote can execute itself against the treasury. Useful — and a reminder that votes follow tokens, so whoever holds the most tokens holds the most power.

Watch forVoter apathy & whale control

06

Wallets & keys

A wallet holds no coins. It holds a private key; your address is derived from its public half, and the balance lives on the chain. Signing with the key is the only way to move anything — which is why the 12-word recovery phrase is the money.

RuleNever share a seed phrase

Layer 2

Ethereum is slow and expensive on purpose. Rollups like Arbitrum, Optimism and Base batch thousands of transactions off-chain, then post a compressed proof back to Ethereum — cheap fees, while still inheriting Ethereum's security.

Oracles

A contract cannot see the outside world; it has no internet access. Oracles such as Chainlink push outside data — prices, scores, weather — on-chain so contracts can react to it. Whoever feeds the oracle becomes a point of trust.

Stablecoins

Tokens pegged to a currency, usually the dollar, so people can transact without price swings. Most (USDC, USDT) are backed by reserves at a real company — which quietly reintroduces exactly the kind of trusted third party the system was built to avoid.

04 — Line by line

A whole application, in twenty-six lines

This is a complete, deployable Solidity contract — twenty-six lines including the blank ones: a tip jar that accepts money from anyone and lets only its owner withdraw. Select any highlighted line to read what it really does.

TipJar.sol 
// SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT
pragma solidity ^0.8.24;
 
contract TipJar {
    address public immutable owner;
    mapping(address => uint256) public tipped;
 
    event Tipped(address indexed from, uint256 amount);
 
    constructor() {
        owner = msg.sender;
    }
 
    function tip() external payable {
        require(msg.value > 0, "send something");
        tipped[msg.sender] += msg.value;
        emit Tipped(msg.sender, msg.value);
    }
 
    function withdraw() external {
        require(msg.sender == owner, "not the owner");
        uint256 amount = address(this).balance;
        (bool ok, ) = payable(owner).call{value: amount}("");
        require(ok, "withdraw failed");
    }
}

How to read this

Select a highlighted line on the left. Everything here is public: once this contract is deployed, anyone can read its code, its storage and every tip it has ever received — no API key, no permission, no takedown.

Version lock

Pins the compiler version. Solidity changes meaningfully between releases, and a contract that compiles differently can behave differently — so the version it was audited against gets written into the source.

An address is an identity

address is a 20-byte account — a person's wallet or another contract. immutable means this one is fixed at deployment and can never be changed, not even by the owner. public makes Solidity generate a free read function for it automatically.

Storage that lives forever

A mapping is the contract's database table: address → amount tipped. It is stored on-chain, replicated by every node, and persists between calls. That permanence is exactly why writing to storage costs gas.

Events are the receipt

Contracts cannot push notifications, so they emit events into the transaction log. Apps and block explorers listen for them. Logs are far cheaper than storage, but contracts cannot read their own events back.

Runs exactly once

The constructor executes at deployment and is then discarded. msg.sender is whoever sent the current transaction — here, the person deploying — so the deployer becomes the permanent owner.

Money is a first-class type

payable lets a function receive ETH; without it the transfer reverts. external means only the outside world can call it. Value arrives with the call itself — there is no separate payment step and no card network in between.

All or nothing

require is a guard. If the condition fails the whole transaction is reverted — every state change is rolled back as if it never happened, and the caller still pays for the gas already burned trying.

Access control is manual

There are no logins here. "Only the owner" is something you have to write yourself, on every single function that needs it. Forgetting this one line is one of the most common — and most expensive — bugs in the industry.

Sending ETH, carefully

Modern Solidity sends value with .call rather than the older .transfer, because .transfer caps the gas forwarded and breaks for wallets that are themselves contracts. .call returns a success flag instead of throwing, so it must be checked — hence the require on the next line.

05 — Reality check

Six things "everyone knows" that aren't true

Web3 attracts overclaiming from its fans and lazy dismissal from its critics. Select a card to turn it over.

Myth 01

"Web3 is just cryptocurrency."

If you have seen one Web3 story, it was probably about a coin going up or down.

Flip for the reality →
Reality

Currency was the first application, not the point. The underlying primitive is a shared database with built-in ownership rules — used for provenance, identity, governance, ticketing and settlement. Tokens are the unit of account that makes those records transferable.

← Flip back
Myth 02

"Blockchains are anonymous."

No name, no email, just a string of hex. Surely that means untraceable?

Flip for the reality →
Reality

Pseudonymous, not anonymous — and permanently public. Every transaction you ever make is visible to everyone forever. Link an address to a name once, through an exchange sign-up or a single careless payment, and the entire history behind it is retroactively de-anonymised.

← Flip back
Myth 03

"Buying an NFT means owning the artwork."

You paid for the picture, so the picture is yours. Right?

Flip for the reality →
Reality

You own a token: a record in a contract that points at a file. Copyright stays with the creator unless the licence explicitly transfers it, and the file itself usually sits on IPFS or an ordinary web server. If that host disappears, the token survives and the image does not.

← Flip back
Myth 04

"Blockchains inevitably burn enormous energy."

The one statistic everybody remembers: a single transaction, a household's electricity.

Flip for the reality →
Reality

That is proof of work specifically. When Ethereum switched to proof of stake in September 2022 its energy use fell by roughly 99.95% overnight. Bitcoin still mines, and it is fair to criticise. The consensus mechanism decides the footprint — not the idea of a blockchain.

← Flip back
Myth 05

"A smart contract is a legal contract."

It has the word contract in it, and it is signed. Close enough?

Flip for the reality →
Reality

It is neither smart nor a contract — it is a program that executes automatically. It has no notion of intent, fairness or force majeure, and no judge to appeal to. If the code does something the author never meant, the code still wins. Real agreements increasingly pair the two: code for settlement, law for everything else.

← Flip back
Myth 06

"It's decentralised, so nobody can shut it down."

Thousands of nodes, no company, no off switch.

Flip for the reality →
Reality

Decentralisation is a spectrum, and most projects sit further along it than they claim. The website is hosted somewhere. The wallet talks to the chain through a handful of RPC providers. Many contracts keep an admin key or an upgradeable proxy. Worth asking of any project: what exactly is decentralised, and who still holds the keys?

What just happened

  • No password was created. The page asked your wallet for permission; the wallet returned the address it is willing to reveal. That is the entire handshake.
  • Your key never moved. Private keys never leave the wallet. Websites can only ever ask it to sign something — and you approve or reject each request.
  • Connecting costs nothing. Reading the chain is free and takes no gas. Only transactions that change state are paid for.
  • No legitimate site will ever ask for your seed phrase. Not for support, not to "verify", not to "sync". Anyone who asks is stealing from you. This page never will — it has no field to type one into.
  • Read what you sign. A signature request that grants unlimited spending approval looks almost identical to a harmless one. If a prompt is confusing, reject it — rejecting is always free.

07 — Decoder

The words people use without explaining them

Ten terms that show up in every thread, every whitepaper and every scam.

Address 0x + 40 hex characters

Your public account number, derived from your public key. Sharing it is safe — it is how people send you things. An address that holds code instead of a person behind it is a contract account; both look identical from the outside.

Gas & gwei /ɡas/ · /ˈɡwaɪ/

Gas measures computational work: every operation has a fixed price, so a busy transaction costs more than a simple transfer. You pay for gas in gwei, one billionth of an ETH (1 gwei = 0.000000001 ETH). Since 2021 most of that fee is burned rather than paid to anyone — a deliberate brake on the fee market.

EVM Ethereum Virtual Machine

The shared computer that every node simulates in lockstep. Contract code compiles to EVM bytecode, and because every node runs it identically, they all agree on the result. So many chains copied it that "EVM-compatible" became a feature: the same contract deploys unchanged to Polygon, Base, Arbitrum and dozens of others.

Seed phrase 12 or 24 ordinary words

A human-readable encoding of the master secret your whole wallet is generated from. Type it into a new wallet anywhere in the world and every account comes back. This is why it must go on paper in a drawer, never in a screenshot, a password manager note, or a chat window — and why nobody trustworthy will ever ask you for it.

Node also: RPC provider

A computer running the chain's software, keeping a copy of the ledger and validating every rule. Anyone can run one. Most apps do not — they talk to a hosted node through an RPC endpoint from providers like Infura or Alchemy, which is one of the quiet centralisation points of the whole ecosystem.

Mempool the waiting room

Where transactions sit after you broadcast them and before they are included in a block. It is public, so everyone can see what you are about to do — which is how front-running works: a bot spots your trade, pays a higher fee, and gets in first.

Finality when it is really done

A confirmed transaction is not instantly permanent. Under proof of work it just becomes exponentially harder to reverse with each new block. Ethereum's proof of stake adds explicit finality: after roughly 13 minutes a block is economically irreversible, because undoing it would require destroying billions of dollars of staked ETH.

Testnet e.g. Sepolia

A parallel copy of the network where the coins are worthless and you get them free from a "faucet". It behaves like the real thing, so it is where every developer — and every sensible beginner — should make their first mistakes.

Bridge chain → chain

Chains cannot talk to each other natively, so bridges lock an asset on one chain and mint a representation of it on another. They concentrate enormous value in a single contract, which has made them the most-robbed component in the entire industry — billions lost across a handful of hacks.

Rug pull the exit scam

A project takes money, then the team drains the liquidity and vanishes. Because transfers are irreversible and pseudonymous, there is nobody to call. The tells are consistent: anonymous team, unaudited contract, guaranteed returns, an urgent deadline, and a contract the owner can still change.
